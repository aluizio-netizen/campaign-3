The committee

What is the CND?

The Commission on Narcotic Drugs is the UN's global forum on drug matters — a functional commission of ECOSOC, established in 1946. Since 1991 it has been the governing body of the UNODC (UN Office on Drugs and Crime).

  • Oversees the international drug-control treaties and shapes prevention and treatment policy.
  • Classifies new substances (synthetic opioids and cannabinoids were placed under control in March 2026).
  • 53 Member States elected by ECOSOC · one vote each · simple majority.
  • A supervisory role: it recommends and coordinates, but does not enforce or monitor compliance.

Works alongside the WHO (health) and the INCB — the International Narcotics Control Board.

Agenda

The committee's two topics

Topic 1

Enhancing global cooperation against transnational drug trafficking.

How states cooperate when crime crosses borders — intelligence, ports, precursor chemicals, and justice.

Topic 2

Transitioning rural economies away from illicit crop cultivation.

The socioeconomic root: coca and poppy as income for poor communities — and how to offer viable alternatives.

Key to this committee: in the CND, bloc positions are less rigid — nations that are adversaries elsewhere find common ground here.

Essential vocabulary

Terms you must master

  • Alternative Development — policy that tackles the socioeconomic causes of illicit cultivation through legal rural livelihoods.
  • Illicit Crop Cultivation — farming of coca/poppy, concentrated in isolated areas with limited market access.
  • Hawala — a trust-based, informal money-transfer system operating outside banks.
  • MLA — Mutual Legal Assistance between states to gather evidence and support prosecutions.
  • NPS — New Psychoactive Substances: not yet under international control when they emerge.
  • Precursor Chemicals — chemicals used to make drugs, not inherently illegal.
  • Sequencing — the principle that viable legal livelihoods should come before enforcement.
  • Extradition — surrender of an accused or convicted person from one state to another for prosecution or punishment.
Topic 1

Global cooperation against transnational drug trafficking

Organized crime that connects production, transport, and distribution across borders — increasingly globalized.

Topic 1 · The problem

Why no single nation can solve it

  • Globalization created cross-border chains: precursors in country A, processing in country B, sale in country C.
  • Every step exploits existing laws, enforcement, and infrastructure — but every step is also a point of intervention.
  • Each state regulates its own way (precursors, borders, penalties), and networks exploit the gaps between them.
  • Only cooperation works: intelligence sharing, common port and customs standards, judicial cooperation.

The central question: what should be done now, in 2026, to curb the flow of drugs from country to country?

Topic 1 · Timeline

A century of treaties and criminal adaptation

  • 1912 — Hague Opium Convention: restricts narcotics to medical and scientific use.
  • 1961 — Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs: unifies the system and creates the INCB.
  • 1971 — Convention on Psychotropic Substances broadens control.
  • 1988 — Convention against Illicit Traffic: extradition, MLA, asset seizure, and precursors.
  • 1970s–80s — Medellín Cartel (Pablo Escobar); later Sinaloa, CJNG, ’Ndrangheta, PCC.
  • 1997 — UNODC established.
  • 2009 / 2016 — Declarations linking control to poverty reduction and human rights.
  • 2030 — Development Agenda ties drug policy to reducing inequality.
Topic 1 · Current situation

The cooperation tools we have today

  • UNODC Drugs Monitoring Platform and INTERPOL's I-24/7 — data on routes and networks shared in real time across 196 countries.
  • INCB monitors precursors (Pre-Export Notification Online, PICS, GRIDS) — it has already diverted fentanyl-precursor shipments.
  • But the dynamics vary sharply by region — there is no single solution.

Topic 1 · Regional picture

The Americas: consumption and production

North America
  • Largest consumer market: opioids, cocaine, methamphetamine.
  • Sinaloa and CJNG cartels dominate routes; the fentanyl crisis.
  • The U.S. designates cartels as terrorist organizations; OFAC sanctions laundering and shell companies.
  • U.S.–Mexico tensions at times undermine cooperation.
South America
  • Colombia, Peru, Bolivia: the world's cocaine production hub.
  • Routes head north through Central America or across the Atlantic to Europe.
  • Project CRIMJUST (EU, INTERPOL, UNODC): joint investigation and intelligence.
  • Operation Lionfish III: 357 arrests, 52+ tonnes seized across 13 countries.
Topic 1 · Regional picture

Europe, Asia, Africa & Oceania

  • Europe — destination market; ’Ndrangheta; ports in Belgium and the Netherlands. MAOC-N seized 1,800+ tonnes (2019–24).
  • Asia — Golden Triangle (Myanmar, Laos, Thailand): a record 236 tonnes of methamphetamine seized in 2024. China is a precursor source. Cooperation via the Mekong MOU.
  • Africa — cocaine transit to Europe; weak borders. West Africa Coast Initiative (WACI, 2009).
  • Oceania — geographic isolation; high street prices attract crime. OCO + Australian Border Force monitor maritime routes.
Topic 1 · Trends & debate

Where the crime is heading

Trends
  • Exploitation of legal-trade infrastructure (seaports).
  • Smuggling submarines, encrypted comms, cryptocurrencies, and hawala.
  • A lag between drug innovation and its regulation.
Questions to consider
  • Which forms of intelligence sharing are feasible given sovereignty and differing laws?
  • How to monitor precursors without blocking legal industry?
  • How to share the cost among producers, exporters, and consumers?
  • How to keep enforcement from being politically "weaponized" between rivals?
Topic 2

Transitioning rural economies away from illicit crop cultivation

The foundation that feeds the entire global drug trade begins with an economic choice made by poor communities.

Topic 2 · The problem

Illicit cultivation is, at root, poverty

  • Many rural regions lack infrastructure, credit, and legal agricultural markets.
  • The illicit economy offers immediate, reliable income — becoming the base that supplies the global drug trade.
  • So it is both a criminal and a socioeconomic problem: addressing the root would sharply hamper trafficking.
  • The delegate's challenge: design strategies that cut production AND build robust rural economies.

Afghanistan captures the central dilemma: the ban cut the global opium supply but devastated rural livelihoods — food insecurity and deeper poverty. Enforcement without an alternative causes greater long-term harm.

Topic 2 · Timeline

From ancient crop to illicit commodity

  • Poppy cultivated in Mesopotamia since ~3,400 BCE; coca leaf in Andean ritual use — for millennia, not illicit.
  • 18th–19th centuries: the British Empire industrializes opium in India and exports it to China.
  • 1961 — the Single Convention consolidates the global prohibition.
  • 1998 — UN Special Session adopts Alternative Development as a key strategy.
  • 2013 — Guiding Principles: community participation, land access, market integration, and sequencing.
Topic 2 · Current situation

What countries are trying

  • Colombia — glyphosate fumigation (until 2015) caused environmental harm; the PNIS (2017) enrolled 99,000+ families to voluntarily substitute coca.
  • Bolivia — "social control": permits limited cultivation for traditional use, with cocaleros as partners in regulation.
  • Thailand — the Royal Project is the success story: transition to coffee and macadamia with long-term investment.
  • Myanmar — the 2021 coup revived poppy. West Africa — without credit and roads, illicit cultivation stays more attractive.
Topic 2 · Trends & debate

Lessons and open questions

Trends
  • Programs that work share long-term commitment and community participation; those that fail impose top-down bans.
  • Displacement effect: cutting cultivation in one country pushes it to the neighbor.
  • Climate change shifts where each crop is viable.
Questions to consider
  • What risks arise if alternative development fails or goes unfunded?
  • Which indicators, beyond hectares, measure success?
  • How can international cooperation prevent cross-border displacement?
  • How to integrate environmental sustainability into the crop transition?
Negotiation

Bloc positions

In the CND, blocs are fluid — one country may move between them depending on the topic. Four logics to guide your research:

  • Enforcement-First — tough laws and punishment as the primary tool; fast results, humanitarian cost.
  • Development-Oriented — address root causes; champion alternative development and harm reduction.
  • Producer & Transit — bear disproportionate cost; call for shared responsibility in funding.
  • Consumer-Market — prioritize border security, financial intelligence, and addiction treatment.
